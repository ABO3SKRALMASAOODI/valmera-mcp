# Plan a deadline for a batch of training video edits

A 52-lesson training batch needs 52 **approved** exports. If each lesson takes 14 minutes of initial review and six minutes of final checks, your reviewers need 17 hours and 20 minutes in all. Forty available review minutes a day provides only 13 hours and 20 minutes over 20 workdays, even before the first draft arrives. Increasing edit capacity alone leaves that shortfall in place.

## Test your delivery plan

[Download the training video delivery planner](examples/training-review-capacity/plan-training-video-delivery.html). On GitHub, choose **Download raw file** and open the saved HTML in a browser. No account is required and the calculation runs on your device.

Enter the lessons due, workdays available, daily editorial and review minutes, and typical minutes for each pass. The planner models an editor or team that creates first cuts and makes revisions, and a reviewer or team that checks both the first and final cuts. A handoff made on one workday becomes available to the next stage the following workday. The output shows first cuts and approvals separately, workweek by workweek.

### Worked example for 52 supplied recordings

These are illustrative pilot timings. Delivery counts use a 20-workday deadline with all sources ready on day one. Each first cut takes 35 minutes, review takes 14 minutes, one correction takes 10 minutes and final approval takes six minutes.

| Staffing scenario | Edit minutes/day | Review minutes/day | Approved on time | Full delivery |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Base plan | 210 | 40 | 35 of 52 | Day 29 |
| Double capacity for first cuts and revisions | 420 | 40 | 35 of 52 | Day 29 |
| Reserve another 25 minutes per day for review | 210 | 65 | 52 of 52 | Day 19 |

All three scenarios require the same 17 hours and 20 minutes of actual review work. First cuts and corrections share editor time. Initial reviews and final checks share reviewer time. The model prioritizes finishing lessons already in review or revision and carries unfinished work to the next workday. It assumes consistent effort per lesson and a repeating workday schedule.

## Compare an editing quote against the deadline

| Question for the provider | What to ask for |
| --- | --- |
| How many usable first cuts arrive each week? | A schedule by delivery batch, after ingest and template setup. Mark Studios, for example, describes an overlapping five-lesson batch process. Confirm which steps the quoted dates include. [Mark Studios course editing](https://www.markstudios.com/services/video-editing/course-video-editing) |
| When do reviewers need to respond? | The expected review window, a named approver and one consolidated notes deadline per batch. Cinecraft’s sample training production guide calls out separate peer and stakeholder review time. [Training production guide](https://vendordirectory.td.org/uploads/assets/654/document/BeginnersGuidetoVideoProductioneBook.pdf) |
| How do approvals work after a correction? | Ask for a final MP4 and matching caption file, the retest checklist, and the weekly delivery count. Some courses have a separate accessibility review and a second approval. [Training video review process](https://cops.usdoj.gov/pdf/training/Video_Development_Process_Guide.pdf) |

Record elapsed wait time separately from the minutes your team actively spends in each pass. If an approver can only join on Thursdays, run the pilot to estimate how many finished lessons one Thursday can clear and negotiate delivery dates around that window. If scripts, approvals or source files will arrive in waves, plan each wave around its actual handoff date.

## Scope a two-lesson pilot
