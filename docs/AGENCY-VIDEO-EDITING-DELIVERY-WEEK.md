# Plan a busy week of outsourced video editing for multiple clients

Work backward from when each client needs a usable file. A vendor can meet the first-draft deadline and still leave too little time for your client to approve the cut, for the editor to make revisions, or for your team to check every export. Take the busiest week and one real revision through a pilot before committing to a recurring batch.

## Turn the delivery list into a schedule

Count the clips before you count the downloads. For example, four different cuts requested in two aspect ratios means four editorial decisions and eight files to check. A new hook or a change to the footage is another cut. A second aspect ratio still needs its own review for readable captions and correct framing.

Suppose a marketing agency needs 14 approved MP4s for two clients on Friday. This is a planning example. Use the working hours, review windows and first-cut turnaround each party has agreed to.

| Client | What they need by Friday | Distinct cuts | Files to check |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Client A | Four product clips in vertical and square | 4 | 8 |
| Client B | Two interview excerpts in vertical, square and landscape | 2 | 6 |
| Total | | 6 | 14 |

If the agency reserves one working day each for client feedback, one revision round and final export checks, the team needs the drafts on Tuesday. Allowing one working day for the first cuts would make the footage handoff due Monday. Agree which time zone the dates use. Ask the vendor how many drafts can run simultaneously, whether either client has a fixed review slot, and how the schedule changes if a reviewer misses it.

| Milestone | Illustrative deadline | Done when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Handoff | Mon 17:00 | Each client's source, approved assets and brief are accessible to the editor. |
| First cuts | Tue 17:00 | Each review link shows the right client, cut and version. |
| Consolidated feedback | Wed 17:00 | A reviewer for each client has resolved conflicting notes and marked what stays unchanged. |
| Approved revisions | Thu 17:00 | The responsible reviewer checks each requested change in the new preview and approves it for export. |
| Final downloads | Fri 17:00 | The 14 files have been opened and checked at their intended aspect ratios. |

## Know which exports a late change affects

Give each cut a short ID tied to the client and source. Keep a row for every delivery file and record the approved version it came from. For something such as a speaker's title, log which cuts contain the words in captions or on-screen graphics. That tells you which files must be rebuilt when the title changes, including files you have already downloaded.

| Cut ID | Note | Files affected | Files to keep |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A-03 | Change the job title in the opening graphic. Keep the footage, caption wording and music. | A-03 vertical and square | A-01, A-02 and A-04 after confirming they omit that title. |
| B-02 | Fix the guest's name in captions at 00:17. | B-02 vertical, square and landscape if all three use that caption. | B-01 after checking its captions and credits. |

Treat the old files as superseded in your delivery record and recheck the new files before sharing them. A square export can place a title over a product even when it looked fine in vertical. Check the changed moment and what you asked the editor to keep. ## Use one peak week to scope the pilot

Send the brief for one representative edit, plus its intended export variants. Measure when the first cut arrives, when the reviewer returns feedback, when the revision is approved, and when all files are ready. Ask how the same process would work for the other client in the same week. Include the number of cuts and files, source lengths, review time zone, dates and who can approve each client’s changes when requesting a quote.
